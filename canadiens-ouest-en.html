The book

In 1878 the journalist and MP Joseph Tassé published in Montreal Les Canadiens de l'Ouest, two volumes of biographies of French Canadians settled between the Great Lakes and the Pacific. Volume I tells fifteen lives, from the Great Lakes to the upper Mississippi; volume II tells twenty, from Minnesota to Texas and from Red River to California, including Aubry's (pp. 179-227), this atlas's main source. The 174 places he names are placed on the map below, with the page where he mentions them.

The link with Aubry. Three threads tie these lives directly to Aubry's. Antoine Leroux: on November 7, 1853, at Albuquerque, Whipple consulted Aubry and Leroux together before launching his 35th-parallel expedition (L15). The upper Mississippi: in October 1846 Aubry visited Galena, Prairie du Chien, St. Peter (Mendota) and St. Anthony Falls (L04 · L05 · L06 · L07), where he “met many French Canadians”: Faribault, Provençal, Rainville, the Rolette family and St. Paul's founders around Vital Guérin. Maskinongé: Tassé thanks Father Bois, priest of Aubry's home parish. Other crossings are indirect: Félix St. Vrain, Antoine Leclerc's friend, was the brother of Ceran St. Vrain, Aubry's friend; Auguste Chouteau, who bought into Dubuque's mines, was of the St. Louis Chouteau family; Prudent Beaudry settled in 1852 at Los Angeles (L46), through which Aubry passed in 1853 and 1854.

The fifteen lives of volume I

Charles de Langlade (1729-1800 · Michilimackinac)

Son of Augustin Mouet de Langlade and an Odawa woman, an officer of the French marine, victor over Braddock on the Monongahela (1755), he spent his life between Michilimackinac and Green Bay, of which he is regarded as the first permanent settler. His grandson Augustin Grignon is Tassé's main source.

  • Trois-Rivières · 1668-1703 · Cradle of the Mouet de Langlade family: Pierre Mouet settled there in 1668; Augustin, Charles's father, was born there in 1703. (p. 3-4)
  • Michilimackinac · 1729-1763 · Augustin de Langlade settled there to trade about 1727; Charles was born there and served as an officer, second in command in 1758. On June 4, 1763, after the massacre of the British garrison, he protected the survivors and was given command of the post. (p. 4-5, 24, 49-64)
  • Green Bay · v. 1745-1800 · Augustin and Charles de Langlade moved there about 1745; Charles put down roots and died there. He is regarded as Wisconsin's first permanent settler. (p. 7-8, 67)
  • Fort Duquesne · juillet 1755 · Langlade arrived there with his Odawa warriors in early July 1755, on the eve of Braddock's advance. (p. 12-13)
  • Battle of the Monongahela · 9 juillet 1755 · With Beaujeu, some 250 French and 650 Indigenous warriors surprised Braddock's army on the Monongahela: the victory with which Langlade's name remained linked. (p. 12-17)
  • Montreal · 1757-1760 · Langlade received orders there from Montcalm and Vaudreuil; it was from Montreal that Vaudreuil informed him of the capitulation, in September 1760. (p. 21-23, 37)

Tassé, t. I, p. 1-103.

Jean-Baptiste Cadot (v. 1723-v. 1800 · Batiscan (Quebec))

Last French commander of the fort at Sault Ste. Marie, married to an Ojibwe woman and a respected leader among the Ojibwe; he protected the trader Alexander Henry after the Michilimackinac massacre (1763) and gave his property to his sons in 1796.

  • Sault Ste. Marie · 1750-1800 · Last French commander of the fort (built in 1750), Cadot lived there with his Ojibwe wife; he owned a large house and extensive land there, and gave his property to his sons Jean-Baptiste and Michel on May 24, 1796. (p. 105-115)
  • Michilimackinac · 1756-1768 · Cadot went there in October 1756 with his wife Anastasie; his son Joseph-Marie was the first baptism recorded after the Jesuits left (1768). (p. 111-114)
  • Fond du Lac (Lake Superior) · 5 août 1826 · The Treaty of Fond du Lac granted a section of land to Saugemanqua, widow of Jean-Baptiste Cadot the younger, and to each of her children. (p. 117)

Tassé, t. I, p. 105-132.

Charles Réaume (v. 1751-1821 · Montreal?)

First justice of the peace at Green Bay (about 1790), famous for his scarlet robe and summary justice, then a judge of Brown County (1818); he died alone in his house at Little Kaukauna in the spring of 1821.

  • Green Bay · v. 1790-1818 · Réaume settled there about 1790, when it had barely twenty hearths; the first justice of the peace, he sat in a scarlet robe; in 1818 he became a Brown County judge. (p. 125-134)
  • Little Kaukauna · 1818-1821 · He had his land rights recognized there and built a house, where he was found dead in the spring of 1821, aged seventy. (p. 134)

Tassé, t. I, p. 133-136.

Jacques Porlier (1765-1839 · Montreal)

A seminarian turned trader, he moved to Green Bay in 1791 in the service of Pierre Grignon, Langlade's son-in-law, and was perhaps Wisconsin's first schoolteacher; justice of the peace under British rule (1815), then chief judge of Brown County (1820-1836).

  • Montreal · 1765-1791 · Born in Montreal in 1765, a pupil at the seminary, militia lieutenant in 1791. (p. 137)
  • Green Bay · 1791-1839 · He arrived in 1791, taught Pierre Grignon's children in the evenings, became justice of the peace and militia captain (1815), then chief judge of Brown County (1820); he died there on July 12, 1839. (p. 137-141)
  • St. Croix River · hiver 1793 · He married Marguerite Grésie there, daughter of a Frenchman and a Menominee woman. (p. 138)
  • Sauk Rapids · hiver 1797 · He wintered there to trade with Robert Dickson, together with Joseph Rainville. (p. 138, 294)
  • St. Anthony Falls · 1805 · He had his post above the falls in 1805 and gave information to Lieutenant Pike. (p. 138)

Tassé, t. I, p. 137-141.

Joseph Rolette (1781-1842 · Quebec)

The “king” of Prairie du Chien: a powerful trader who sided with the British in 1812-1814, then an agent of the American Fur Company; he supplied seed wheat to the Red River settlers (1820) and died at Prairie du Chien in 1842. His brother Frédéric distinguished himself on Lake Erie.

  • Quebec · 1781-1803 · Born in Quebec, son of Jean-Joseph Rolette and Angélique Lortie; he left the city for Montreal in October 1803. (p. 144-146)
  • Prairie du Chien · 1806-1842 · Settled at Prairie du Chien, he received Pike there in April 1806, supplied British troops in 1814, dominated the trade for the American Fur Company and died there on December 1, 1842. (p. 147-208)
  • Capture of Michilimackinac · juillet 1812 · Rolette took part in the capture of the American fort on Mackinac Island, without a cannon shot. (p. 150-151)
  • Lake Erie (Frédéric Rolette) · 1812-1813 · His brother Frédéric commanded the brig Hunter (1812), fought at the River Raisin (January 1813) and in the Battle of Lake Erie (September 10, 1813); he died at Quebec in 1831. (p. 160-163)
  • Red River · 1820 · After the grasshoppers of 1818 and 1819, the Red River settlers bought 250 bushels of seed wheat from Rolette, brought back in June 1820. (p. 169)

Tassé, t. I, p. 143-211.

Salomon Juneau (1793-1856 · L'Assomption (Quebec))

Born at L'Assomption, founder of Milwaukee, where he settled in the fall of 1818 as a trader; the city's first mayor (1846), later settled at Theresa, Dodge County. Gabriel Franchère paid tribute to him at his death (1856).

  • L'Assomption · 9 août 1793 · Birth of Laurent-Salomon Juneau, son of François Juneau dit Latulippe and Thérèse Galarneau. (p. 216)
  • Milwaukee · 1818-1856 · Juneau settled on the Milwaukee River in the fall of 1818, bought 130 acres on the east bank in 1835, became the city's first mayor and saw it grow to nearly 100,000 people. Laurent Ducharme had opened a trading post there as early as 1777. (p. 213-235)
  • Theresa (Dodge County) · après 1848 · Juneau went to settle at Theresa, in Dodge County, whose county seat now bears his name (Juneau). (p. 227-237)

Tassé, t. I, p. 213-237.

Julien Dubuque (1762-1810 · Saint-Pierre-les-Becquets (Quebec))

Born at Saint-Pierre-les-Becquets, settled at Prairie du Chien by 1785, he obtained from the Meskwaki (1788) the lead mines that bear his name, had them recognized by Spain (1796), sold half to Auguste Chouteau (1804) and died in 1810; the city of Dubuque grew from his settlement.

  • Saint-Pierre-les-Becquets · 10 janvier 1762 · Baptism of Julien Dubuque, of a family from Rouen (Jean Dubuc, married at Quebec in 1668). (p. 239-240)
  • Prairie du Chien · 1785-1788 · He was settled there by 1785 with Basile Giard and Pierre Antaya; on September 22, 1788, a great council granted him seven leagues of frontage on the Mississippi. (p. 240-243)
  • Dubuque's Mines (Mines of Spain) · 1788-1810 · He worked the Meskwaki lead mines, had his grant confirmed by Carondelet (1796), sold half to Auguste Chouteau (1804), received Pike (1805-1806) and died in the spring of 1810; his tomb overlooks the river. (p. 243-253)
  • St. Louis (Auguste Chouteau) · 1804-1810 · Auguste Chouteau of St. Louis acquired half of Dubuque's lands in 1804 and had the mine auctioned after his death. (p. 246, 255)
  • City of Dubuque · 1833-1878 · The city that grew from his settlement had about 18,000 people in 1870, including a few hundred French Canadians. (p. 261-262)

Tassé, t. I, p. 239-262.

Antoine Leclerc (v. 1785-1861 · unknown)

Interpreter at the great Western treaties (St. Louis and Prairie du Chien, 1825; Fort Armstrong, 1832 and 1836). Settled at the ville à Mallet (Peoria) in 1809, then at Rock Island in 1813, he received from the Sauk and Meskwaki the land on which Davenport stands; a town and a county in Iowa bear his name.

  • Ville à Mallet (Peoria) · 1809-1813 · Leclerc settled there in 1809, among Canadian hunters and voyageurs; the destruction of the village drove him away in 1813. (p. 265-266)
  • Rock Island (Fort Armstrong) · 1813-1836 · He settled on the then-deserted island in 1813; he served as interpreter there for the treaties of September 21, 1832 and September 28, 1836. His friend Félix St. Vrain, Indian agent at Rock Island, was killed during the Black Hawk War (1832). (p. 266-273)
  • St. Louis (treaties) · 2-3 juin 1825 · Interpreter at the treaties with the Osage (June 2) and the Kansa (June 3, 1825). (p. 268)
  • Prairie du Chien (treaty) · 24 juin 1825 · Interpreter at the great treaty with the Ojibwe, Odawa and Potawatomi. (p. 268)
  • Davenport · 1832-1861 · On the land given him by the Sauk and Meskwaki stands Davenport, a city of over 20,000 people by 1878. (p. 270-274)
  • LeClaire (Iowa) · 1878 · A town and a county in Iowa bear his name. (p. 266)

Tassé, t. I, p. 263-274.

Jacques Duperon Baby (v. 1731-1789 · Montreal)

A Detroit merchant, friend and supplier of the region's nations, he lived through Pontiac's siege (1763) and died at Sandwich in 1789; his family was among the most influential in Upper Canada.

  • Detroit · 1760-1796 · Married at Detroit on November 20, 1760 to Suzanne Réaume, he lived through Pontiac's siege (1763), the only regular siege ever conducted by Indigenous nations; his widow left Detroit for Quebec in 1796. (p. 277-290)
  • Sandwich (Windsor) · 1789 · Baby died at Sandwich about August 2, 1789. (p. 290)

Tassé, t. I, p. 275-292.

Joseph Rainville (Renville) (v. 1779-1846 · Minnesota)

A Métis born near St. Paul, coureur de bois, interpreter for Pike (1805) and Major Long (1823), commander of the Dakota for the British in 1812; settled at Lac qui Parle, where he sowed the first wheat on the upper Mississippi. A Minnesota county bears his name.

  • Near St. Paul · v. 1779 · Birth of Joseph Rainville “in the wilderness,” a little below St. Paul. (p. 293)
  • Mendota (Fort St. Peter) · 1808-1823 · Pike's interpreter, recommended in a letter dated Mendota (1808); in 1823 he guided Major Long's expedition, which left Fort St. Peter on July 7. (p. 294-299)
  • Lac qui Parle · 1826-1846 · He spent the rest of his days there, sowed the first wheat on the upper Mississippi and raised sheep and cattle by the hundred; his house, Nicollet said, was the only refuge for travellers. (p. 299-301)

Tassé, t. I, p. 293-304.

Louis Provençal (?-1855 · unknown)

Trader at Traverse des Sioux from the start of the century, unlettered but shrewd, he kept his accounts in signs only he could read; he died at Mendota in 1855.

  • Traverse des Sioux · v. 1800-1850 · Provençal traded there from the beginning of the century. (p. 305)
  • Coteau des Prairies · XIXe s. · His son, a trader at Coteau des Prairies, was killed there by Dakota warriors. (p. 308)
  • Mendota · 1855 · He ended his days there in 1855. (p. 308)

Tassé, t. I, p. 305-307.

Jean-Baptiste Faribault (1775-1860 · Berthier (Quebec))

He left Montreal in 1796 for the North West Company, traded in Minnesota, met Pike (1805), settled at Prairie du Chien, then on Pike Island and at Mendota; member for Mendota in Minnesota's first legislature (1849). The town of Faribault bears his son's name.

  • Berthier · 1775 · His father Barthélemy, born in Paris, settled at Berthier as a notary after the Conquest; Jean-Baptiste was born there in 1775. (p. 309-310)
  • Montreal · juin 1796 · He left Montreal for Michilimackinac with three young Canadians and two North West Company agents. (p. 314)
  • Prairie du Chien · v. 1808-1819 · He traded there for several years before yielding to Colonel Leavenworth's urging. (p. 322-324)
  • Pike Island · 1820 · He settled on the island, near the future Fort Snelling, and farmed it; the 1820 treaty recognized the island to his wife and descendants. (p. 324)
  • Mendota · 1826-1849 · He moved his household to Mendota, which he represented in 1849 in Minnesota's first legislature; in 1850 he was a commissioner for peace between the Dakota and Ojibwe. (p. 325-333)
  • Faribault (Minnesota) · 1878 · The town bears the name of his son Alexander, its founder. (p. 325-331)

Tassé, t. I, p. 309-332.

Jean-Baptiste Lefebvre (XIXe s. · Canada)

Guide and voyageur, Schoolcraft's companion on Lake Superior, he settled at Superior City in 1853 with Basile Saint-Denis, François Roy and Jean-Baptiste Saint-Jean.

  • Superior City · 1853 · He settled there in 1853 with Basile Saint-Denis, François Roy and Jean-Baptiste Saint-Jean; the following year thousands of speculators flocked there. (p. 333-334)

Tassé, t. I, p. 333-336.

Jean-Baptiste Perrault (1760-1844 · Trois-Rivières)

Son of an inspector at Trois-Rivières, he left in 1783 to trade in the Illinois Country, then spent sixty years in the Northwest fur trade; he died at Sault Ste. Marie in 1844.

  • Trois-Rivières · 1760-1783 · Son of a Saint-Maurice ironworks employee who became an inspector at Trois-Rivières; he left for the Illinois Country in 1783. (p. 337)
  • Fond du Lac · 1784-1785 · He accompanied Mr. Kay's poorly supplied expedition in the Fond du Lac region. (p. 338-340)
  • Sault Ste. Marie · 1844 · Schoolcraft met him there about 1828; he died there on November 12, 1844, aged eighty-five. (p. 340)

Tassé, t. I, p. 337-340.

Jean-Marie Ducharme (1723-1807 · Lachine (Quebec))

A Lachine trader, imprisoned by the Americans in 1775-1776, he led an attack on (then Spanish) St. Louis in 1780, then retired to Lachine about 1800. His brother Laurent had set up the first trading post at Milwaukee in 1777.

  • Lachine · 1775-1807 · He was living there when the Americans invaded Canada (1775); convicted of selling provisions to their troops, he spent eleven months in prison; he returned to live there about 1800. (p. 342-349)
  • Milwaukee (Laurent Ducharme) · 1777 · Laurent Ducharme set up the first known trading post there in 1777. (p. 216)
  • Attack on St. Louis · 26 mai 1780 · Ducharme led warriors against St. Louis, then Spanish; the attack was repulsed. (p. 346-347)

Tassé, t. I, p. 341-350.

The twenty lives of volume II

Vital Guérin (1812-1870 · Saint-Rémi (Quebec))

A voyageur in Gabriel Franchère's service (1832), he settled in 1839 on the site of St. Paul, ploughed its first furrow and gave the land for its first church; Tassé makes him the Canadian pioneer of Minnesota's capital and lists the Canadians who joined him there.

  • Saint-Rémi · 17 juillet 1812 · Birth of Vital Guérin, son of a voyageur. (p. 3)
  • Fort Snelling · 1838-1840 · Canadian families driven out of Red River settled near the fort; Major Plympton expelled them from the military reserve, and they founded St. Paul. (p. 7)
  • St. Paul · 1839-1870 · He settled in October 1839 on land abandoned by Phelan, was the first to plough the future city's soil (1841), gave the land for St. Paul's chapel and died there on November 11, 1870. (p. 4)
  • Mendota · 26 janvier 1841 · Marriage of Guérin and Adèle Perry. (p. 11)
  • Gervais Lake (Little Canada) · v. 1844 · Benjamin Gervais founded another French settlement eight miles north of St. Paul, on a lake that bears his name. (p. 10)

Tassé, t. II, p. 1-30.

Joseph Rolette fils (v. 1820-1870 · Prairie du Chien)

Son of the “king” of Prairie du Chien (volume I), a trader at Pembina and Minnesota legislator; in 1857 he blocked the move of the capital from St. Paul to St. Peter by vanishing with the bill. He supported the Red River insurrection (1869) and died at Pembina in 1870.

  • Prairie du Chien · v. 1820 · Birth of Joseph Rolette Jr., the “king of the frontier.” (p. 32)
  • Cincinnati · années 1830 · He spent three years at school in Cincinnati. (p. 33)
  • Pembina · 1840-1870 · He led the cart trains between Pembina and Red River, represented Pembina in the Minnesota legislature (1853-1857), travelling there by dog sled, and died there on May 16, 1870. (p. 40)
  • Souris River (Dakota) · années 1840 · He traded with the Métis, Cree, Ojibwe and Dakota. (p. 33)
  • St. Paul (Fuller House) · février 1857 · He vanished with the bill moving the capital to St. Peter until the session ended: St. Paul remained the capital. (p. 36)
  • St. Peter · 1857 · The town that was to become Minnesota's capital. (p. 35)

Tassé, t. II, p. 31-40.

Jean-Baptiste Mallet (v. 1773-v. 1801 · Michilimackinac)

Founder of the “ville à Mallet,” the origin of Peoria (Illinois), he led Canadians from Peoria and Cahokia to capture the British fort at St. Joseph on Lake Michigan; the village was razed in 1812 by American militia.

  • Michilimackinac · v. 1773 · Birth of Jean-Baptiste Mallet. (p. 42)
  • Ville à Mallet (Peoria) · 1778-1812 · Mallet founded a village a mile and a half from the old French settlement on Lake Peoria; Antoine Leclerc, Michel Lacroix and dozens of Canadians lived there until militia destroyed it in 1812. (p. 43)
  • Fort St. Joseph (Lake Michigan) · 1777-1778 · The British post was taken in 1777 by Brady with sixteen Canadians from Cahokia and Peoria, then again by the expedition that set out from Peoria. (p. 49)

Tassé, t. II, p. 41-54.

Pierre Ménard (1766-1844 · Richelieu valley (Quebec))

A trader at Vincennes and then Kaskaskia, partner of Manuel Lisa (1808), president of the Illinois Territory's legislative council, then the State's first lieutenant governor (1818-1822); as an Indian treaty commissioner he named Keokuk (1826).

  • Richelieu valley · 1766 · Birth of Pierre Ménard (the parish name is illegible in the scanned copy; tradition places it at Saint-Antoine-sur-Richelieu). (p. 56)
  • Vincennes · v. 1786 · Ménard went there first and supplied the armies of Clark and Scott. (p. 57)
  • Kaskaskia · 1790-1844 · Settled at Kaskaskia, he presided over the legislative council there (first session November 15, 1810), became lieutenant governor of Illinois (1818-1822) and received Lafayette (1825). (p. 61)
  • Apple Creek · 5 novembre 1798 · He asked the Spanish authorities for a twenty-arpent grant at the mouth of the creek. (p. 59)
  • St. Louis (Manuel Lisa & Co.) · 1808 · He joined Manuel Lisa's Missouri fur company. (p. 58)
  • Keokuk (Lower Rapids) · 4 juillet 1826 · With Lewis Cass, he gave the town growing at the foot of the rapids the name Keokuk. (p. 59)

Tassé, t. II, p. 55-72.

François Ménard (v. 1778-1833 · Quebec)

Pierre's brother, who left Quebec in 1795 for Kaskaskia with his brother Hippolyte, he ran river trade on the Mississippi down to New Orleans and died at Kaskaskia in 1833.

  • Quebec · 1795 · François and Hippolyte Ménard left Quebec to join their brother at Kaskaskia. (p. 73)
  • Kaskaskia · 1795-1833 · He died there on September 28, 1833. (p. 81)

Tassé, t. II, p. 73-82.

Jean-Baptiste Beaubien (v. 1785-1863 · Detroit)

From a Detroit family originally from Batiscan, he traded at Milwaukee before Juneau, then settled at Chicago, where in 1817 he bought the American Fur Company house near Fort Dearborn; Tassé makes him one of Chicago's first citizens.

  • Batiscan · 1709 · Birth of Jean-Baptiste Cuillerier dit Beaubien, ancestor of the Detroit family. (p. 84)
  • Detroit · 1742-v. 1800 · His grandfather married at Detroit (1742); Jean-Baptiste was born there (the date is poorly legible in the scanned copy). (p. 84)
  • Milwaukee · v. 1800-1810 · He traded on Lake Michigan where Milwaukee would rise, several years before Juneau. (p. 85)
  • Chicago (Fort Dearborn) · 1817-1840 · In 1817 he bought the American Fur Company house south of the fort (rebuilt in 1816), and founded a debating club there (1831-1832). (p. 89)
  • Chicago (Sauganash Hotel) · 1831 · His brother Marc kept the Sauganash Hotel on the Chicago River, named after Billy Caldwell. (p. 89)

Tassé, t. II, p. 83-98.

Noël Levasseur (1799-1879 · Saint-Michel-d'Yamaska (Quebec))

He left Montreal in 1817 to trade in Wisconsin, in Astor's service, and settled in 1837 at Bourbonnais, Illinois, which he made the hub of Canadian immigration towards Kankakee and Sainte-Anne.

  • Saint-Michel-d'Yamaska · 1799 · Birth of Noël Levasseur, on Christmas night. (p. 99)
  • Montreal · 15 mai 1817 · He left Montreal in a barge carrying eighty engagés. (p. 100)
  • Fond du Lac (Wisconsin) · v. 1818 · Leaving Michilimackinac for the American Fur Company, he wintered and built a post at Fond du Lac. (p. 100)
  • Chicago (treaty) · 26 septembre 1833 · The Treaty of Chicago with the Odawa, Ojibwe and Potawatomi settled claims, including that of his wife Josephte Chevalier. (p. 105)
  • Iroquois River · 1834-1837 · He traded three years “at the Iroquois” before selling up. (p. 105)
  • Bourbonnais · 1837-1879 · He bought 1,500 arpents and drew thousands of Canadians; Bourbonnais became the largest Canadian parish in Illinois (Clerics of St. Viator college, Congrégation convent). (p. 105)
  • St. Anne (Illinois) · 1851-1852 · Chiniquy led hundreds of Canadian families there, fifteen miles from Bourbonnais. (p. 110)

Tassé, t. II, p. 99-118.

Joseph Robidou (1783-1868 · St. Louis)

Born at St. Louis to a Canadian father, an American Fur Company trader at the Blacksnake Hills, he founded there the city of St. Joseph (Missouri), which he laid out in 1843.

  • St. Louis · 2 août 1783 · Birth of Joseph Robidou; his house at Main and Myrtle, inherited from his father, hosted Missouri's first general assembly in 1812. (p. 121)
  • Blacksnake Hills (St. Joseph) · 1803-1868 · He kept a post there (visited by Prince Maximilian on April 24, 1833), hosted the county's first court (1839), obtained his patents (1843) and laid out the town of St. Joseph, with 600 people by 1845. (p. 121)

Tassé, t. II, p. 119-130.

Jean-Baptiste-Louis Roy (XIXe s. · unknown)

Defender of the blockhouse at Côte-Sans-Dessein on the Missouri, besieged in 1814 by the Sauk, Meskwaki and Ioway; the young men of St. Louis presented him with a rifle of honour.

  • Côte-Sans-Dessein · 1814 · Siege of the blockhouse by the Sauk, Meskwaki and Ioway; Roy and a handful of defenders held out for four days. (p. 132)

Tassé, t. II, p. 131-136.

Louis-Vital Bogy (Baugy) (1813-1877 · Ste. Genevieve (Missouri))

Son of a Canadian from Les Cèdres who came by way of New Orleans, a St. Louis lawyer, promoter of the Iron Mountain railroad, Commissioner of Indian Affairs (1866), then senator for Missouri (1873-1877).

  • Beauport · 1638 · François Baugy came to Canada; his descendants settled at Beauport. (p. 137)
  • Les Cèdres · XVIIIe s. · Joseph Baugy, the father, left Les Cèdres for the Illinois Country. (p. 138)
  • New Orleans · v. 1786-1805 · The father settled there under Spanish rule before moving up to Ste. Genevieve about 1805. (p. 139)
  • Ste. Genevieve · 1805-1835 · Birth of Louis-Vital (April 9, 1813); he returned as a lawyer in 1835. (p. 141)
  • Kaskaskia · 1832-1833 · He read law with Judge Nathan Pope, interrupted by the Black Hawk War (1832). (p. 142)
  • Pilot Knob (iron mountain) · 1848 · With others he bought the Pilot Knob iron mountain and promoted the railroad linking it to St. Louis. (p. 148)
  • St. Louis · 1840-1873 · Lawyer and Democratic politician, member of the Missouri legislature (1840), candidate for Congress (1863). (p. 150)
  • Washington · 1866-1877 · Commissioner of Indian Affairs (1866), senator for Missouri (1873); he died on September 20, 1877 of an illness contracted in Washington. (p. 151)

Tassé, t. II, p. 137-168.

Jacques Fournier (1752 ?-1871 · near Quebec)

A voyageur reputed to be over a hundred, who died in Kansas in 1871: a scout at Ticonderoga (1775), trapper on the Niagara, at Pittsburgh and New Orleans, soldier at Lundy's Lane. Tassé himself doubts the age attributed to him.

  • Near Quebec · 1752 ? · He was reportedly born near Quebec in 1752 (or at Rivière-du-Sud in 1767, by his own account). (p. 170)
  • Lake Champlain (Ticonderoga) · 1772-1775 · Settled on Lake Champlain and a scout for the fort, he was captured by Ethan Allen in May 1775. (p. 170)
  • Niagara River · 1793 · He trapped there. (p. 170)
  • Pittsburgh (Fort Duquesne) · fin XVIIIe s. · By way of Presque Isle (Erie), he walked to the old Fort Duquesne. (p. 171)
  • New Orleans · jusqu'en 1812 · He lived there until the War of 1812. (p. 172)
  • Lundy's Lane · 25 juillet 1814 · Wounded twice and commended by General Scott. (p. 170)
  • Kansas City (the bluffs) · 15 mai 1871 · Retired to the Kansas City bluffs, where old trappers called him “Pino,” he died in his garden on May 15, 1871; the papers gave his age as 124 or 125. He is Hafen's “Old Pino” Fournaise (vol. 8, p. 175). (p. 169, 175)

Tassé, t. II, p. 169-174.

Michel-Branamour Ménard (1805-1856 · Laprairie (Quebec))

Nephew of Pierre Ménard, a trader at Detroit and Kaskaskia, then in Texas: a signer of the declaration of independence (1836), member for Galveston and founder of that city, where he died in 1856.

  • Laprairie · 5 décembre 1805 · Birth of Michel-Branamour Ménard. (p. 175)
  • Detroit · v. 1821 · At sixteen he joined the American Fur Company. (p. 175)
  • Kaskaskia · v. 1824 · At his uncle Pierre's request, he traded at Kaskaskia. (p. 175)
  • Nacogdoches · 1833-1834 · He traded there with Mexicans and Indigenous peoples. (p. 176)
  • Washington-on-the-Brazos · mars 1836 · Member of the convention that proclaimed Texas independence. (p. 177)
  • Galveston · 1836-1856 · He bought the Galveston site from Congress, founded the town, represented it (1838) and died there in 1856. (p. 177)

Tassé, t. II, p. 175-178.

François-Xavier Aubry (1824-1854 · Maskinongé (Quebec))

The subject of this atlas. Tassé's chapter (pp. 179-227), the atlas's main source, is followed by Aubry's 1853 journal as an appendix (pp. 385-401). Tassé adds that Aubry brought three of his brothers to St. Louis in 1851 to be educated, and that Whipple consulted him at Albuquerque in 1853 together with Antoine Leroux.

  • Maskinongé (Saint-Justin) · 1824 · Birth of Aubry; Tassé thanks Father Bois, priest of Maskinongé. See atlas record L01. (p. 179)
  • St. Louis · 1851-1854 · Aubry brought his brothers Joseph, André and Auguste there to study; they went home to Canada after his death. See record L99. (p. 226)
  • Albuquerque · novembre 1853 · Whipple, organizing his 35th-parallel expedition, consulted Aubry and Antoine Leroux. See record L15. (p. 242)
  • Santa Fe · 18 août 1854 · His death caused indescribable excitement in Santa Fe. See record L11. (p. 223)

Tassé, t. II, p. 179-227.

Antoine Leroux (v. 1801-1861 · St. Louis?)

A trapper settled at Taos, the most famous guide in New Mexico: he led Cooke's battalion to California (1846-1847), then the Sitgreaves (1851), Gunnison and Whipple (1853-1854) expeditions; Whipple consulted him together with Aubry at Albuquerque.

  • Taos · 1822-1861 · His home, a few days' journey north of Santa Fe. (p. 242)
  • San Diego · 1846-1852 · End of the march of Cooke's battalion, which he guided (1846-1847); he met Bartlett there on April 24, 1852. (p. 238)
  • Hopi villages · 1850 · According to W. A. Bell, he was the first “American” to visit the seven villages. (p. 247)
  • Zuni · septembre 1851 · Starting point of the Sitgreaves expedition to the Colorado. (p. 234)
  • Camp Yuma · 30 novembre 1851 · The starving expedition reached the camp near the mouth of the Gila. (p. 237)
  • Verde River · 1851 · He crossed the Verde River. (p. 248)
  • Albuquerque · 7 novembre 1853 · Whipple consulted him together with Aubry, then hired him as guide. (p. 242)
  • 38th-parallel route (Cochetopa Pass) · 1853 · Gunnison's scout for part of the route (indicative position). (p. 241)
  • Los Angeles · 20 mars 1854 · End of the Whipple expedition. (p. 244)

Tassé, t. II, p. 229-248.

Prudent Beaudry (1816-1893 · Sainte-Anne-des-Plaines (Quebec))

A merchant in San Francisco (1850) and then Los Angeles (1852), he built the city's waterworks and hillside districts and became its mayor (1874-1876); he endowed a scholarship at Montreal's École polytechnique (1875).

  • Sainte-Anne-des-Plaines · 1816 · Home of the Beaudry brothers; Tassé gives there the birth of his brother Victor (February 22, 1831). (p. 260)
  • San Francisco · 1850-1852 · With his brother Victor, he opened a syrup factory. (p. 258)
  • Los Angeles · 1852-1893 · Settled there in April 1852, he built reservoirs and an aqueduct (1874), developed the hills and became the city's mayor. (p. 256)
  • Montreal · 1875-1876 · He endowed a scholarship at the École polytechnique (May 26, 1875) and married in Montreal (April 1876). (p. 260)

Tassé, t. II, p. 249-260.

Gabriel Franchère (1786-1863 · Montreal)

A clerk on Astor's expedition, he sailed from New York on the Tonquin (1810), helped found Astoria (1811) and returned overland (1814); his narrative is a classic. American Fur Company agent at Sault Ste. Marie, then in New York, he died at St. Paul in 1863.

  • Montreal · 1786-1810 · Born November 3, 1786; he left Montreal on July 26, 1810 and returned in 1814. (p. 264)
  • New York · 6 septembre 1810 · The Tonquin sailed for the Northwest Coast; later Franchère was American Fur Company agent there. (p. 267)
  • Sandwich Islands (Hawaii) · février 1811 · Port of call for the Tonquin. (p. 273)
  • Astoria · 1811-1814 · Founding of the post at the mouth of the Columbia; he left it on April 4, 1814. (p. 273)
  • Fort William · 1814 · A stage of his overland return. (p. 293)
  • Sault Ste. Marie · 1834-1837 · American Fur Company agent; his wife died there (July 5, 1837). Vital Guérin was in his service in 1832. (p. 294)
  • St. Paul · 1863 · He died at the home of his son-in-law John S. Prince, mayor of St. Paul: the last survivor of Astoria. (p. 298)

Tassé, t. II, p. 261-298.

Pierre-Chrysologue Pambrun (1792-1841 · L'Islet (Quebec))

A militia officer in 1812, a Hudson's Bay Company clerk caught up in the Red River troubles (1816), then commander of Fort Babine (New Caledonia) and of Fort Walla Walla (1832-1841), where he received Bonneville and the missionaries.

  • L'Islet · 17 décembre 1792 · Birth of Pierre-Chrysologue Pambrun. (p. 299)
  • Vaudreuil · 1812 · His father's letter from Vaudreuil to the young militia officer. (p. 300)
  • Fort Douglas (Red River) · mai 1816 · He left the fort on May 12, 1816 for Qu'Appelle; his convoy was seized by the Métis a few weeks before Seven Oaks. (p. 303)
  • Qu'Appelle · 1816 · Hudson's Bay Company post. (p. 303)
  • Fort Babine (New Caledonia) · v. 1825-1827 · He took command of the fort. (p. 304)
  • Fort Walla Walla · 1832-1841 · Commander of the fort and friend of the Indigenous people, whom he taught to farm; he received Bonneville, Wyeth and Father Demers (1839). (p. 311)
  • Waiilatpu (Whitman mission) · 1841 · Dr. Whitman was called to his bedside; he would be killed in 1847. (p. 317)

Tassé, t. II, p. 299-320.

Joseph Larocque (v. 1787-1866 · Canada)

A North West Company clerk on the Columbia, commander of the Kamloops (She-Whaps) post in 1812, he reached Astoria in April 1813 with news of the war; chief trader after 1821, he funded the St. Joseph academy on the Willamette (1843) and ended his days with the Grey Nuns in Ottawa.

  • She-Whaps (Kamloops) · 1812 · He commanded the post alongside the Astor company's agent. (p. 325)
  • Astoria · 11 avril 1813 · With MacTavish, he brought Astoria news of the war and of a British frigate on its way. (p. 326)
  • Rainy Lake · 31 juillet 1817 · Meeting of North West Company partners. (p. 333)
  • Willamette (St. Paul, Oregon) · octobre 1843 · He funded the St. Joseph academy, staffed by French Canadian teachers. (p. 310)
  • Ottawa · 1857-1866 · He lived with the Grey Nuns and died there on December 1, 1866. (p. 337)

Tassé, t. II, p. 321-338.

Pierre Falcon (1793-1876 · Elbow Fort (Swan River))

The “bard of Red River,” present at the Battle of Seven Oaks (June 19, 1816), on which he composed his famous song; settled at White Horse Plain.

  • Elbow Fort (Swan River) · 4 juin 1793 · Birth of Pierre Falcon (indicative position). (p. 349)
  • Seven Oaks · 19 juin 1816 · He was present at the clash in which Governor Semple died, and made a song of it. (p. 346)
  • White Horse Plain · v. 1825-1876 · He settled there and was still living there in 1878. (p. 350)

Louis Riel père (1817-1864 · Île-à-la-Crosse)

A Métis born at Île-à-la-Crosse and raised in Lower Canada, he led the Red River Métis at the 1849 trial of Guillaume Sayer and broke the Hudson's Bay Company monopoly; he died at Saint-Boniface in 1864. His son Louis would lead the 1869-1870 rising.

  • Île-à-la-Crosse · 7 juin 1817 · Birth of Louis Riel Sr. (p. 353)
  • Berthier · 1822 · Brought to Canada, he received the baptismal ceremonies there. (p. 353)
  • Fort Garry (Sayer trial) · 17 mai 1849 · He led the armed Métis to the trial of Guillaume Sayer: the fur-trade monopoly collapsed. (p. 370)
  • Two Rivers (near Pembina) · 1858 · On the prairie he met his son Louis, bound for the college in Montreal; he never saw him again. (p. 378)
  • Saint-Boniface · 21 janvier 1864 · He died there. (p. 378)

Tassé, t. II, p. 353-380.

Settlements in the introduction (volume I)

The introduction (pp. v-xlv) surveys the French and Canadian settlements of the West: the chain of New France posts, the Illinois villages, Detroit, then the Canadian communities of 1878, from Illinois to Minnesota and as far as New Mexico.

  • Kaskaskia · First settlement of the Illinois Country; Father Gibault was its priest from 1770 to 1789.
  • Cahokia · One of the French villages of the Illinois Country.
  • Prairie du Rocher · French village of the Illinois Country, near Fort de Chartres.
  • Fort de Chartres · Mississippi fort, one of the links between Louisiana and New France.
  • Sainte-Geneviève · Founded by Illinois settlers who crossed to the west bank after 1763.
  • St. Louis · Founded by Illinois settlers after 1763; Tassé lists it among the great cities born of French settlements.
  • St. Charles · French settlement in Missouri.
  • Carondelet · French settlement in Missouri.
  • Vincennes · Post on the Ouabache (Wabash).
  • Detroit · Founded in 1701; after the Illinois, the most numerous and prosperous colony.
  • Bourbonnais · At the heart of Illinois's compact Canadian population in 1878 (with Manteno, Sainte-Anne, l'Érable, Kankakee).
  • Chicago · A city born of a French post; the Laframboise family still lived there.
  • St. Paul · By 1878 some 20,000 Canadians lived in Minnesota, mostly at St. Paul and St. Anthony Falls.
  • Peoria · Founded by Jean-Baptiste Mallet (the “ville à Mallet”).
  • Maskinongé · Tassé thanks Father Bois, priest of Maskinongé: Aubry's home parish, whose biography appears in volume II.
  • Santa Fe · Tassé thanks Bishop Lamy of Santa Fe for his information; he notes Canadians as far as New Mexico.
