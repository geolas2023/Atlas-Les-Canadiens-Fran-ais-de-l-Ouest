From lost continent to rediscovered archipelago

In 1979 the geographers Dean Louder, Christian Morissonneau and Eric Waddell proposed reading French America not as an empire lost in 1763 but as an archipelago: French-speaking islands scattered from Hudson Bay to Louisiana by generations of mobile people, coureurs de bois then voyageurs, guides, interpreters and teamsters, whose mule trains ran from Texas to the Assiniboine until the railroad came. Aubry, a Canadian from Maskinongé who became a Santa Fe freighter, is one of these trajectories. This page maps one of the densest islands, Illinois, and its Midwest cousins.

Ties with Aubry. The Mallet brothers opened the road to Santa Fe from the Illinois Country as early as 1739, a century before him. Antoine Le Claire, a resident of La Ville de Maillet, is the Leclerc of Tassé's volume I. Noël Levasseur, founder of Bourbonnais, is in volume II; Tassé himself visited Bourbonnais. The Canadian families of Red Lake Falls came from Maskinongé County. And at Pine Ridge, descendants of John Baptiste Richard, the Platte trader in Hafen's series, carry on French names. The Kankakee “Little Canada”

For the historian Edward Kantowicz (1982), it was in the countryside rather than the cities that French Canada managed to transplant itself in the Midwest. After François Bourbonnais (1835), Noël Levasseur, twenty years with the American Fur Co., settled on the Kankakee in 1837; his tales of fertile prairie, carried back to Quebec, set off a chain migration. In 1857 a Province of Canada parliamentary inquiry found it easier for a habitant to get land a thousand miles away in Illinois than twenty miles into the Quebec bush. Two forces shook the colony in the 1850s: Chiniquy, the “apostle of temperance” turned schismatic, and the Illinois Central, which created Kankakee and bypassed Bourbonnais. Yet the colony persisted: eight parishes in Kankakee County, five in Chicago, churches of local sandstone modelled on Quebec's, and St. Viator College at the centre.

The Kankakee centennial souvenir (1953) adds the prehistory: Shaw-waw-nas-see's Potawatomi, Gurdon Hubbard's Bunkum post turned over to Levasseur in 1832, the Treaty of Camp Tippecanoe, and, a century later, the Dandurands, Papineaus, Braults, Rivards and LaPlantes acting out the town's history in the great centennial pageant.

The French press of Illinois (1854-1931)

L.-P. Cormier (1957) distinguishes two eras: that of Alexandre Grandpré, whose Kankakee paper, republican and at first anticlerical, became the Courrier de l'Illinois based in Chicago (1857-1896); then that of Louis Bachand-Vertefeuille, who merged the Chicago, St. Paul and Minneapolis papers and in 1903 attempted the only French daily in the West. In 1876 the Courrier addressed 40,000 French-speaking voters in Illinois.

St. Viator College in 1900-1901

The college's thirty-third annual catalogue recounts its founding (the Clerics of St. Viator from Joliette at Bourbonnais in 1865, Father Thomas Roy's classical course in 1868, the 1874 charter, the Roy memorial chapel of 1889) and lists the year's students with their home. At the head of the college: Fathers P. Beaudoin, C. Fournier, L.-A. Sénécal and E.-L. Rivard.

The French-speaking archipelago of the Midwest today

The three issues of the Initiatives in French Midwest magazine (University of North Dakota, 2009-2012) survey the memory places of this archipelago: St. John the Baptist parish in Duluth, the Baumier center in Marquette, Turtle Mountain and its Michif Métis, Rolette County, the Canadian villages of Red Lake Falls, Terrebonne and Gentilly, the “French” Oglala of Pine Ridge, Saint-Boniface and its Festival du Voyageur, Voyageurs National Park. Among the articles: Pierre Bottineau, Louis Riel, Joseph Rolette, Father Jean Malo, the sieur du Lhut, Father Hennepin.
